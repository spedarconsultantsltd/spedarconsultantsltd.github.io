Forest fragmentation and the importance of landscape connectivity

Environmental ResearchGIS & Remote Sensing2 November 20263 min readSPEDAR Consultants
General educational insight prepared by SPEDAR Consultants. It summarises published sources listed below and does not report SPEDAR project findings.

When forest cover statistics are reported, the headline figure is usually area: how many hectares remain, how many were lost. Area matters, but it hides a second, equally important question. Is the remaining forest one continuous block, or hundreds of small patches separated by farmland, roads and settlements? Two landscapes with identical forest area can behave very differently for wildlife, water and people.

Habitat loss and fragmentation are not the same thing

Ecologists distinguish between habitat loss, the reduction in total habitat area, and fragmentation per se, the breaking apart of habitat into more, smaller and more isolated pieces. In practice the two usually occur together, which is why they are often confused. Fahrig's widely cited review (2003) argued that loss typically has the larger and more consistent effect on biodiversity, while the effects of fragmentation itself vary by species and context. For planning, the practical lesson is to measure both rather than treating forest area as a sufficient indicator.

Why edges matter

Fragmentation multiplies edges. Forest edges are exposed to more light, wind and heat, are easier to access for grazing, fuel-wood collection and hunting, and are more readily colonised by invasive species. A global analysis by Haddad and colleagues (2015) estimated that around 70% of the world's remaining forest lies within one kilometre of a forest edge, and summarised experimental evidence that fragmentation can reduce biodiversity and impair ecosystem functions over time.

Measuring fragmentation from satellite data

Most fragmentation analysis starts with a forest/non-forest map derived from satellite imagery, then quantifies the spatial pattern of the forest class. Commonly used approaches include:

  • Landscape metrics such as number of patches, patch density, mean patch area, edge density and core area, popularised by the FRAGSTATS software (McGarigal & Marks, 1995).
  • Forest fragmentation classes that label each forest pixel as interior, edge, perforated or patch according to the forest share of its neighbourhood (Riitters et al., 2000).
  • Morphological Spatial Pattern Analysis (MSPA), which classifies forest into core, edge, perforation, islet, bridge and branch elements using image morphology (Soille & Vogt, 2009). Bridges are particularly useful because they identify structural links between core areas.

Results are sensitive to analytical choices: image resolution, the minimum mapping unit, the definition of forest (canopy cover threshold, treatment of plantations), and the edge width used. Reporting these choices is essential if results are to be compared across years or studies.

From structure to connectivity

Landscape connectivity describes the degree to which a landscape facilitates or impedes movement between resource patches (Taylor et al., 1993). Structural connectivity is read directly from the map: are patches physically joined? Functional connectivity asks whether a particular species can actually move through the intervening land uses. Functional approaches typically build a resistance surface, in which each land-cover type is assigned a cost of movement, and then model the easiest routes between habitat patches using least-cost paths or circuit theory (McRae et al., 2008). Circuit-theory outputs are helpful for identifying pinch points, narrow areas where movement is concentrated and where loss of a small area could disconnect a network.

Relevance to the Chittagong Hill Tracts

The hill forests of south-eastern Bangladesh are among the country's most biologically important ecosystems and have been under sustained pressure from land-use change. A study of Bandarban Sadar Upazila by Mamnun and Hossen (2021), using classified satellite imagery from 1988 to 2018, reported substantial forest loss and rising edge density, and identified agro-horticultural plantation as a principal driver of reduced landscape diversity. Studies like this show that pattern-based indicators can reveal degradation that area totals alone would understate.

Connectivity analysis adds a forward-looking dimension. Once corridors and pinch points are mapped, they can inform where restoration or protection would deliver the greatest benefit for the least land, a practical consideration in landscapes where forest, agriculture and livelihoods overlap.

Good practice for applied studies

  • Use consistent imagery and seasons across time periods, and document cloud masking and classification methods.
  • Assess classification accuracy against independent reference data before computing metrics.
  • Report several complementary metrics rather than a single index.
  • Treat resistance values as assumptions to be tested, ideally with species occurrence data or expert review, and run sensitivity analyses.
  • Involve local communities and forest managers when interpreting results and identifying restoration priorities.

Fragmentation and connectivity analysis does not replace field ecology, but it provides a consistent, repeatable way to see how a landscape is changing and where intervention is likely to matter most.

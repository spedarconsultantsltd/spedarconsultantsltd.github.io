How GIS and remote sensing support evidence-based planning

GIS & Remote SensingUrban & Regional Planning26 October 20263 min readSPEDAR Consultants
General educational insight prepared by SPEDAR Consultants. It summarises published sources listed below and does not report SPEDAR project findings.

Planning decisions depend on knowing where things are, how they are changing and who is affected. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) provide the framework for organising and analysing that spatial information; remote sensing supplies much of the raw observation, particularly where ground data are sparse, out of date or expensive to collect.

What has changed: open, frequent satellite data

The European Space Agency's Sentinel-2 mission illustrates how much the baseline has shifted. Its multispectral instrument records 13 spectral bands, with four visible and near-infrared bands at 10 m resolution, six red-edge and shortwave-infrared bands at 20 m, and three atmospheric bands at 60 m, across a 290 km swath. With two satellites in orbit the mission revisits the equator every five days; Sentinel-2A launched in 2015, Sentinel-2B in 2017 and Sentinel-2C in September 2024 (ESA). Combined with the Landsat archive, which extends back to the 1970s, planners can now build consistent multi-year records for most of the world at no data cost.

Cloud-computing platforms such as Google Earth Engine (Gorelick et al., 2017) make it practical to process these archives without downloading terabytes of imagery, which lowers the barrier for smaller teams and public agencies.

A note on NDVI

The Normalised Difference Vegetation Index is one of the most widely used remote-sensing indicators (Rouse et al., 1974; Tucker, 1979). It exploits the contrast between strong near-infrared reflectance and strong red absorption by healthy vegetation:

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)    For Sentinel-2: NDVI = (B8 − B4) / (B8 + B4)

Values range from −1 to +1; water is usually negative, bare soil and built surfaces are near zero, and dense green vegetation is typically well above 0.5. NDVI is useful but not a direct measure of biomass or forest condition: it saturates over dense canopy, is affected by soil background and atmospheric conditions, and varies with season. It works best as one input among several.

Accuracy is part of the result

A land-cover map is an estimate, not a census. Good practice, set out by Olofsson et al. (2014), is to assess accuracy using an independent, statistically designed reference sample, to report an error matrix with user's and producer's accuracy, and to estimate class areas with confidence intervals rather than simply counting mapped pixels. Change maps deserve particular care, because small classification errors in each date can compound into large apparent changes.

Practical limits in Bangladesh

  • Monsoon cloud cover limits optical imagery for several months each year. Radar data (for example Sentinel-1) can see through cloud and is especially useful for flood mapping.
  • Small, mixed land parcels in rural areas can fall below the 10 m pixel scale, so very-high-resolution imagery or field data may be needed.
  • Administrative and cadastral data such as mouza maps vary in currency and georeferencing quality, which affects any analysis that links land cover to ownership or governance units.

Communicating results

The most rigorous analysis has little effect if decision-makers cannot read it. Clear cartography, plain-language summaries, explicit statements of uncertainty, and interactive maps that let users explore their own area all help evidence travel from a technical report into a planning decision.

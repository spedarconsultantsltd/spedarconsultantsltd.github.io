Forest fragmentation and connectivity in the Chattogram Hill Tracts

A research collaboration announced with the Institute of Forestry and Environmental Sciences, University of Chittagong, studying forest change in Lama Upazila between 2015 and 2025.

The hill forests of the Chattogram Hill Tracts support exceptional biodiversity and the livelihoods of many communities, but face continuing pressure from land-use change. Forest area alone does not capture that pressure. How the remaining forest is broken up, and whether patches remain connected, determines whether wildlife can move, whether ecosystems remain functional, and where restoration would do most good.

The study applies GIS and satellite remote sensing to map forest cover over a ten-year period, assess fragmentation patterns and identify areas that may serve as ecological corridors.

Research objectives

  1. Map forest cover and its change in Lama Upazila between 2015 and 2025 using Sentinel-2 imagery.
  2. Assess vegetation condition using NDVI and related spectral indicators.
  3. Quantify forest fragmentation using landscape pattern metrics.
  4. Evaluate structural and functional ecological connectivity between forest patches.
  5. Identify potential restoration corridors to inform biodiversity conservation.

Research themes and capabilities

Areas where our team has relevant methods and experience. Expand each theme for typical methods.

Land-use and land-cover change

Capability: research theme and methods

Multi-temporal classification of satellite imagery to quantify how land is being converted, for example from forest or agriculture to settlement, and to support land-use planning and environmental monitoring.

Typical methods

  • Sentinel-2 and Landsat time series
  • Supervised and object-based classification
  • Accuracy assessment and area estimation
  • Change matrices and transition maps

Climate and disaster vulnerability

Capability: research theme and methods

Combining hazard, exposure and socioeconomic data to map where communities and assets face the greatest climate risk, at upazila, union or settlement level.

Typical methods

  • Indicator selection and weighting
  • Flood, drought and erosion layers
  • Household and community surveys
  • Vulnerability indices and maps

Urban heat and green infrastructure

Capability: research theme and methods

Using land surface temperature, vegetation indices and land-use data to understand urban heat patterns and the cooling role of parks, trees and water bodies.

Typical methods

  • LST retrieval from thermal imagery
  • NDVI–LST relationships
  • Green space accessibility
  • Planning recommendations

River and coastal change

Capability: research theme and methods

Tracking shoreline and riverbank movement over time to inform planning for erosion-affected communities, infrastructure and coastal ecosystems.

Typical methods

  • Shoreline extraction
  • Erosion and accretion rates
  • Char and riverbank settlement mapping
  • Field verification

Transport and accessibility

Capability: research theme and methods

Spatial and survey-based analysis of mobility, service access and travel demand to support transport and facility planning.

Typical methods

  • Network analysis
  • Travel and passenger surveys
  • Service catchments
  • Demand estimation

How a geospatial study comes together

The sequence we follow for remote-sensing-led research, adapted to each question.

  1. Define and scope

    Agree questions, study area, time period and the decisions the results should inform.

  2. Acquire and prepare data

    Select cloud-free imagery, correct and mask it, and assemble ancillary and field data.

  3. Classify and validate

    Produce maps and indices, then test them against independent reference samples.

  4. Analyse patterns

    Measure change, fragmentation, risk or access, and examine drivers with local evidence.

  5. Interpret and share

    Report findings with uncertainty, clear maps and practical recommendations.
